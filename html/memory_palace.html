Memory Palace Journey

The Method of Loci (Greek journey method) is the most powerful memorization technique ever devised. Ancient orators used it to deliver hours-long speeches from memory. The principle: attach abstract concepts to vivid, bizarre scenes at familiar physical locations. The more absurd and sensory the image, the stronger the memory.

This page maps the entire Staff SDE curriculum onto a building you will walk through mentally. Each room contains stations -- specific objects or locations -- and each station encodes a key concept through a vivid scene.

The Building Floor Plan

Your memory palace is a two-story building. Memorize the layout, then walk through it daily.

Memory Palace Floor Plan
Walking Order

Always walk the same route: Front Door (Mental Models) → Living Room (Algorithm Patterns) → Kitchen (Sorting) → Dining Room (Data Structures) → Study (Systems Design) → Workshop (Software Engineering) → Server Room (CS Fundamentals) → Upstairs Office (Staff-Level Skills). Same order, every time.

Front Door Mental Models

You stand at the front door. Six objects hang on or near the door. These are the 6 mental model shifts you must internalize before entering the house.

Front Door -- 6 Mental Model Stations
1

Light Switch vs. Sunrise

A light switch on the wall. Flip it -- instant light. Next to it, a tiny window showing a sunrise gradually brightening. Both produce light, but via different paths.

Deterministic vs. Eventually Consistent. Hardware: global clock, instant propagation. Software: no global clock, eventual convergence.

Why it works: The physical switch gives you immediate, deterministic feedback -- just like a synchronous bus. The sunrise takes time to reach full brightness -- just like eventual consistency.

2

Lego Blocks vs. ASIC Chip

On the doorstep, colorful Lego blocks labeled "DB", "Cache", "Auth" snap together easily. Beside them, a single complex chip with 200 pins sits alone -- impressive but immovable.

Composition over Construction. Staff engineers compose proven components; they do not build everything from scratch.

Why it works: Legos are modular, reconfigurable, and labeled. The ASIC is powerful but fixed. Software should be Legos.

3

Tangled Wire vs. Breadboard

A ball of tangled wire hanging on a nail -- it works but nobody can trace a signal. Below it, a neat breadboard with color-coded, labeled traces. A happy face drawn next to the breadboard, a confused face next to the tangle.

Optimization-First vs. Clarity-First. Code is read 10x more than written. Clarity beats cleverness.

Why it works: You have debugged tangled wires before. You know which one you would rather inherit.

4

Traffic Light

A miniature traffic light mounted next to the door. Red, yellow, green -- with arrows showing the transition order. It never goes from red to green without passing through its defined transitions.

State Machines Everywhere. Use explicit enums with defined transitions, not boolean flag soup.

Why it works: Traffic lights are the universal state machine. Every software object has a lifecycle just like this.

5

Layer Cake

A cross-section of geological layers in a frame on the wall. Bottom to top: Hardware, OS, Runtime, Framework, App, User. Each layer is a different color. A drill tries to go straight through -- it cracks everything.

Abstraction Layers. Each layer talks only to the layer directly below. Never skip layers.

Why it works: Like a PCB stackup -- you never route a signal through the wrong layer.

6

Three Bridges

Three parallel bridges over a small stream by the doorstep. One bridge has collapsed (red X). Traffic flows smoothly over the other two. A sign reads: "Redundancy saves lives."

Failure is Normal. Design for failure with retries, fallbacks, circuit breakers, and redundancy.

Why it works: Like redundant power supplies -- you expect one to fail, so you have three.

Living Room Algorithm Patterns (16)

The largest room in the house. You spend the most time here. Each of the 16 algorithm patterns is mapped to a specific object or location in this room. Walk clockwise from the window.

Living Room -- 16 Pattern Stations
1

The Window (Sliding Window)

A large window with curtains that slide open and close. Through the glass, a continuous stream of numbers flows past like a news ticker. The curtain frame is exactly 3 numbers wide -- it slides right, one number enters, one exits.

Sliding Window: Maintain a window of fixed or variable size over a contiguous subarray. Move right edge to expand, left edge to shrink.

A literal sliding window. The curtains are your left and right pointers.

2

The Couch (Two Pointers)

Two people sit at opposite ends of the couch. They stand up and walk toward each other, one step at a time. They meet in the middle, shake hands, and sit back down.

Two Pointers: Place pointers at both ends of a sorted array and walk them inward. Use for pair-sum, palindrome checking, container problems.

Two people, opposite ends, walking toward each other. That is the algorithm.

3

The Bookshelf (Binary Search)

A tall bookshelf with books sorted by author. You always open to the middle shelf first. Too high alphabetically? Look at the left half. Too low? Look at the right half. You never scan linearly.

Binary Search: Halve the search space each step. O(log n). Works on any sorted or monotonic structure.

A sorted bookshelf is the canonical binary search example. Always start in the middle.

4

The Fish Tank (BFS)

A stone drops into the fish tank. Ripples spread outward in concentric circles -- level by level, each ring reaches all points at the same distance before the next ring forms.

BFS: Explore level by level using a queue. Finds shortest unweighted path. Think concentric ripples.

Ripples are BFS -- they expand uniformly outward, covering each distance level before moving to the next.

5

The Cat (DFS)

A curious cat exploring the room. It goes deep into one corner, sniffs behind every object, hits a wall, then backs out and tries another path. It never explores broadly -- always commits to one path until it dead-ends.

DFS: Explore as deep as possible before backtracking. Uses a stack (or recursion). Think: cat exploring corners.

A cat's exploration strategy is pure DFS -- commit to one direction, hit a wall, backtrack.

6

Christmas Lights (Topological Sort)

A chain of Christmas lights strung along the wall. You plug them in from the power source outward. Each light can only turn on after the one before it is on. One light is burned out -- everything after it is dark.

Topological Sort: Order nodes so every edge u->v has u before v. Think: prerequisite chains, build systems, course schedules.

Each light depends on the previous one -- a dependency chain. The order matters.

7

Coffee Table Spreadsheet (DP)

A giant spreadsheet on the coffee table. Each cell's value depends on the cells above and to the left. You fill it methodically from the top-left corner, row by row. No cell is computed twice.

Dynamic Programming: Break into overlapping subproblems. Store results in a table. Build bottom-up from base cases.

DP IS a table. Each cell depends on previously computed cells. The spreadsheet IS the algorithm.

8

The Buffet (Greedy)

Someone at a buffet cart. They always grab the best-looking dish available RIGHT NOW. No planning ahead, no considering what is coming next on the cart. Just take the best current option every time.

Greedy: At each step, make the locally optimal choice. Works when local optima lead to global optimum (interval scheduling, Huffman, Kruskal).

A greedy eater at a buffet -- no strategy, just "what looks best right now?"

9

The Maze (Backtracking)

A maze drawn on the wall. A mouse enters, goes straight, hits a dead end, backs up to the last intersection, tries another path. It explores every possibility systematically, pruning dead ends.

Backtracking: Try a choice, recurse. If it fails, undo the choice and try the next option. Prune branches early to avoid exponential blowup.

A maze is the physical embodiment of backtracking. Dead end = prune. Back up = backtrack.

10

The Trophy Shelf (Heap)

A pyramid-shaped trophy shelf. The biggest trophy is always on top. When you add a new trophy, it starts at the bottom and bubbles up to its correct position. Remove the top trophy, and the next-biggest floats up.

Heap: Priority queue. O(log n) insert/extract. The root is always the min or max. Think: top-K, merge-K, median tracking.

A trophy shelf that self-organizes -- biggest always on top. That is a max-heap.

11

The Plate Stack (Monotonic Stack)

A stack of plates, strictly decreasing in size from bottom to top. When a bigger plate arrives, it crushes and removes all smaller plates on top. Only plates smaller than the new one survive.

Monotonic Stack: Maintain a stack where elements are in sorted order. Pop elements that violate the monotonic property. Solves: next greater element, histogram area, stock span.

The crushing visual is unforgettable. Big plate arrives, small plates get destroyed -- that is the pop operation.

12

Photo Albums (Union Find)

Family photo albums on the coffee table. Two families get married -- their albums merge into one. To find if two photos are in the same album, you trace back to the album cover (root). Path compression: everyone points directly to the cover.

Union Find: Track connected components. Union merges two sets. Find traces an element to its root. Near-O(1) with path compression + union by rank.

Merging photo albums = union. Finding which album a photo belongs to = find.

13

Phone Tree Chart (Trie)

An old telephone tree chart on the wall. Each level is a letter. You follow the path to spell words: C-A-T goes left-left-right. Multiple words share prefixes. The tree fans out like a phone tree.

Trie: Prefix tree. Each path from root to node spells a prefix. O(L) lookup where L = word length. Used for autocomplete, spell check, IP routing.

A telephone tree is a trie -- each branch is a character, shared prefixes share paths.

14

The Odometer (Prefix Sum)

A running odometer display on the dashboard of a toy car. To find the distance between two points, just subtract the readings: distance(A,B) = reading(B) - reading(A). No need to remeasure.

Prefix Sum: Precompute cumulative sums. Any range sum in O(1): sum(i..j) = prefix[j+1] - prefix[i]. Works in 2D too (integral images).

An odometer IS a prefix sum. Distance = difference of readings. No re-counting.

15

The Coat Rack (Hash Map)

A coat rack with numbered hooks. Each coat has a label. You compute the hook number from the label (hash function). Hook 7 = blue coat. Look up any coat in O(1) by computing its hook number.

Hash Map: O(1) lookup/insert/delete. Map keys to values via hash function. Foundation of: two-sum, frequency counting, grouping, deduplication.

A coat rack with labeled hooks. The label-to-hook mapping IS the hash function.

16

The GPS Device (Dijkstra)

A GPS navigation device sitting on the side table. It shows the shortest route, but as you discover new roads (process neighbors), it updates: "Found a shorter path! Recalculating..." It always processes the closest unvisited node next.

Dijkstra: Shortest path in weighted graphs with non-negative edges. Uses a min-heap. Always processes the nearest unvisited node. O((V+E) log V).

GPS navigation IS Dijkstra. It explores closest nodes first and updates when shorter paths are found.

Kitchen Sorting Algorithms

Everything in the kitchen involves organizing things. Sorting is organizing data. Each station maps a sorting algorithm to a kitchen activity.

Kitchen -- 6 Sorting Stations
1

Boiling Pot (Bubble Sort)

Bubbles rising in a boiling pot. The largest bubbles rise to the surface first. Each pass through the water pushes the next-largest bubble to the top.

Bubble Sort: O(n^2). Repeatedly swap adjacent elements if out of order. Largest elements "bubble" to the end. Simple but slow.

2

Chef Scanning (Selection Sort)

A chef scanning all ingredients on the counter, picking the smallest one, placing it at the start. Then scanning the remaining ingredients, picking the smallest again. Methodical, thorough, slow.

Selection Sort: O(n^2). Find the minimum in the unsorted portion, place it at the correct position. Easy to understand, not efficient.

3

Card Hand (Insertion Sort)

Sorting a hand of playing cards as they are dealt. Each new card is inserted into the correct position in the already-sorted hand. You shift cards right to make room.

Insertion Sort: O(n^2) worst, O(n) best (nearly sorted). Insert each element into its sorted position. Great for small or nearly-sorted data.

4

Two Conveyors (Merge Sort)

Two conveyor belts, each carrying sorted items. They merge into one output belt: always take the smaller item from whichever belt has the smaller front item. Divide, conquer, merge.

Merge Sort: O(n log n) always. Divide in half, sort each, merge. Stable. Great for linked lists and external sorting. Uses O(n) extra space.

5

The Bouncer (Quick Sort)

A bouncer at the kitchen door. Everyone shorter than the bouncer goes left, everyone taller goes right. Then a new bouncer appears in each group and repeats. Divide around a pivot.

Quick Sort: O(n log n) average, O(n^2) worst. Pick a pivot, partition around it. In-place. Usually fastest in practice.

6

Orange Pyramid (Heap Sort)

A pyramid of oranges. Take the top orange (the largest), set it aside. The pyramid reorganizes -- the next-largest floats to the top. Repeat until all oranges are sorted in a line.

Heap Sort: O(n log n) always. Build a max-heap, repeatedly extract the max. In-place, not stable. Guaranteed performance.

Dining Room Data Structures

Where data is served and consumed. Each piece of furniture holds data in a specific way.

1

The Long Table (Array)

A long dining table with numbered place settings. Each seat is adjacent, indexed 0 to n-1. You can find any seat by number instantly, but inserting a new place in the middle means everyone shifts.

Array: O(1) random access, O(n) insert/delete in the middle. Contiguous memory. Foundation of everything.

2

Conga Line (Linked List)

Guests in a conga line. Each person holds the shoulders of the person in front. To insert someone, just break one connection and splice them in. But finding the 50th person means walking from the front.

Linked List: O(1) insert/delete at known position, O(n) access. Dynamic size, no shifting. Useful for LRU caches, adjacency lists.

3

Stacked Plates (Stack)

A stack of dinner plates. Last plate placed on top is the first one taken off. You cannot reach the bottom plate without removing all plates above it. LIFO -- Last In, First Out.

Stack: LIFO. O(1) push/pop. Used in: DFS, expression evaluation, undo operations, parenthesis matching, call stacks.

4

Serving Line (Queue)

Guests line up at the buffet. First person in line is first served. New guests join at the back. FIFO -- First In, First Out. Nobody cuts the line.

Queue: FIFO. O(1) enqueue/dequeue. Used in: BFS, task scheduling, message passing, rate limiting.

5

Family Tree Portrait (Tree/BST)

A large family tree portrait on the dining room wall. Grandparents at top, parents below, children at bottom. Each person has at most two children. The left child's name comes alphabetically before the parent.

Binary Search Tree: O(log n) search/insert/delete when balanced. Left subtree smaller, right subtree larger. Foundation for sets and maps.

6

Seating Chart (Graph)

A complex seating chart where lines connect people who know each other. Some connections are one-way (directed), some have weights (how well they know each other). The chart itself is a graph -- nodes are people, edges are relationships.

Graph: Nodes + edges. Adjacency list or matrix. Foundation for BFS, DFS, shortest path, connected components, topological sort.

Study / Library Systems Design

Where you read, plan, and architect. Each station maps to a systems design concept.

1

Globe on Desk (CAP Theorem)

A globe with three continents highlighted: C, A, P. A sign on the desk says "Pick any two." You can spin the globe but can never see all three at once -- one is always hidden.

CAP Theorem: Consistency, Availability, Partition Tolerance -- pick two. In a network partition, choose CP (consistent but unavailable) or AP (available but inconsistent).

2

Filing Cabinet (Database Scaling)

A filing cabinet that is overflowing. Solution 1: Buy a bigger cabinet (vertical scaling). Solution 2: Split files across multiple cabinets by letter (sharding). The labels A-M go to cabinet 1, N-Z to cabinet 2.

Database Scaling: Vertical (bigger machine) vs. horizontal (sharding). Replication for reads. Partitioning for writes.

3

Sticky Notes (Caching)

Sticky notes on the monitor with frequently-used phone numbers. Faster than looking up the phone book every time. But the note might be outdated if someone changed their number -- cache invalidation.

Caching: Store frequently accessed data closer to the consumer. Trade staleness for speed. The two hard things: cache invalidation and naming things.

4

Inbox Tray (Message Queues)

A physical inbox tray on the desk. Letters pile up. A clerk processes them one at a time. If the clerk is busy, letters just wait in the tray -- nothing is lost. Producer-consumer decoupling.

Message Queues: Decouple producers and consumers. Handle backpressure. Enable async processing. Kafka, RabbitMQ, SQS.

Workshop / Garage Software Engineering

Where you build things with tools. Each station maps to a software engineering practice.

1

Workbench (SOLID Principles)

Five tools hanging on the pegboard above the workbench, each labeled with a letter: S, O, L, I, D. Single-wrench, Open-socket, Liskov-pliers, Interface-separator, Dependency-inverter.

SOLID: Five principles for maintainable OO code. Single Responsibility, Open/Closed, Liskov Substitution, Interface Segregation, Dependency Inversion.

2

Assembly Line (CI/CD)

A model assembly line on the shelf. Raw materials enter one end, pass through testing stations, quality checks, and a finished product comes out the other end automatically. Break the line, red light flashes.

CI/CD: Automated build, test, deploy pipeline. Every commit triggers the assembly line. Broken tests halt deployment.

3

Testing Pyramid Poster (Testing)

A poster on the wall showing a pyramid. Wide base of unit tests (fast, many). Middle layer of integration tests (moderate). Tiny peak of E2E tests (slow, few). An inverted pyramid next to it with a red X -- that is the anti-pattern.

Testing Pyramid: Many unit tests (fast, focused), fewer integration tests, fewest E2E tests. Invert the pyramid and everything is slow and brittle.

4

Version Control Shelf (Git)

A shelf with branching model trains. The main track is stable. Side tracks (branches) diverge, get tested, then merge back. If a side track derails, the main track is unaffected.

Git Branching: Feature branches isolate work. PRs with reviews merge back. Trunk-based development for speed, gitflow for releases.

Server Room / Basement CS Fundamentals

The infrastructure underneath everything. Dark, humming, full of machines. Every concept here is foundational.

1

Server Rack (Processes & Threads)

A server rack with multiple blades. Each blade is a process -- isolated, with its own memory. Within each blade, multiple LEDs blink independently -- those are threads sharing the same blade's memory.

Processes vs. Threads: Processes are isolated (separate memory). Threads share memory within a process. Like separate boards vs. parallel circuits on one board.

2

Network Cables (TCP/UDP)

Two types of network cables. The thick, shielded cable (TCP) guarantees delivery with confirmation receipts. The thin cable (UDP) is faster but packets might get lost -- like shouting across the room vs. handing a signed letter.

TCP vs. UDP: TCP = reliable, ordered, connection-based. UDP = fast, unreliable, connectionless. TCP for data, UDP for streaming/gaming.

3

Deadlock Puzzle (Concurrency)

Two robot arms reaching for the same two bolts. Arm A grabs bolt 1 and waits for bolt 2. Arm B grabs bolt 2 and waits for bolt 1. Neither can proceed. A deadlock -- freeze forever.

Concurrency Hazards: Deadlock (circular wait), race conditions (unsynchronized access), starvation (never scheduled). Use locks carefully, prefer message-passing.

4

Database Console (SQL & ACID)

A glowing green terminal showing SQL queries. On the wall: "A-C-I-D" in large letters. Atomicity (all or nothing), Consistency (rules enforced), Isolation (transactions don't interfere), Durability (committed = permanent).

ACID: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability. The guarantees that make databases trustworthy. Like signal integrity guarantees on a bus.

Upstairs Office Staff-Level Skills

You climb the stairs to get here -- literally elevated perspective. These are the skills that separate senior from staff engineers.

1

Whiteboard (RFC / Design Docs)

A large whiteboard covered with a structured document: Problem, Context, Options (with pros/cons), Decision, Consequences. The format is always the same. Below it: stacks of past RFCs, all following the same template.

RFC Writing: Staff engineers write before they code. Problem statement, constraints, options analysis, decision with rationale, rollback plan.

2

Conference Phone (Cross-Team Influence)

A conference phone on the desk with multiple lines lit up -- each line is a different team. You are not their manager, but you influence through clarity, data, and earned credibility. No authority needed.

Influence Without Authority: Staff engineers lead through technical credibility, clear communication, and shared context. Not through org chart power.

3

Dashboard Monitor (Observability)

Three monitors on the desk: one showing logs (text streams), one showing metrics (graphs and gauges), one showing traces (request flow through services). Together they give complete system visibility.

Observability: Logs (what happened), Metrics (how much), Traces (the path). Like having an oscilloscope, multimeter, and logic analyzer -- all three together.

4

Mentor's Chair (Teaching & Mentoring)

A comfortable chair across from a smaller desk. This is where you sit with junior engineers, explaining not just what to do but why. Your multiplier effect: make 5 engineers 20% better rather than doing 100% more yourself.

Mentoring: Staff engineers multiply team output. Teach the reasoning, not just the answer. Create systems that make others successful.

How to Use the Memory Palace

Daily Practice (5 minutes)

Every morning, close your eyes and walk through the building:

  1. Front Door: Touch each of the 6 objects. Can you name all 6 mental models?
  2. Living Room: Walk clockwise. Window, couch, bookshelf, fish tank, cat... Can you name all 21 patterns?
  3. Kitchen: Stove, counter, cards, conveyors, bouncer, oranges. All 6 sorting algorithms?
  4. Other rooms: Scan each station. Can you recall the concept?

The first time will take 15 minutes. By day 7, it will take 3 minutes. By day 30, it will be instant.

When You Encounter a Problem

When you see a coding problem, mentally walk to the Living Room and scan the stations. Ask: "Does this problem look like something at one of my 16 stations?" The vivid images should trigger pattern recognition faster than memorized rules.

Making It Stronger
  • Add sensory details: What does the boiling pot sound like? What does the buffet smell like?
  • Make scenes absurd: The cat is wearing a tiny headlamp while exploring. The GPS device is arguing with you.
  • Personalize: Use your actual house, office, or childhood home instead of this generic building.
  • Add motion: The sliding window curtains are flapping in the wind. The two people on the couch are racing to meet.

Practice: Pattern Recognition via the Palace

For each problem description below, mentally walk to the Living Room, scan the stations, and identify which pattern matches. Click "Reveal" to check your answer.

1. Given a string, find the length of the longest substring without repeating characters.

Station 1: The Window (Sliding Window) -- Expand window right, shrink from left when a repeat is found. The window frame is your substring.

2. Given a sorted array, find two numbers that add up to a target sum.

Station 2: The Couch (Two Pointers) -- Two people at opposite ends of the couch. Sum too large? Right person steps left. Sum too small? Left person steps right.

3. Given a list of courses and their prerequisites, find a valid order to take all courses.

Station 6: Christmas Lights (Topological Sort) -- Each course depends on its prerequisite, like each light depends on the one before it. Find the order from the power source outward.

4. Given a grid, find the shortest path from top-left to bottom-right.

Station 4: The Fish Tank (BFS) -- Ripples spreading outward level by level. BFS guarantees the shortest unweighted path.

5. Given a set of coins, find the minimum number of coins to make a target amount.

Station 7: Coffee Table Spreadsheet (DP) -- Each cell dp[amount] depends on previously computed cells dp[amount - coin]. Fill the table from 0 to target.

6. Given n queen pieces, place them on an n x n chessboard so no two queens attack each other.

Station 9: The Maze (Backtracking) -- Place a queen, recurse. If invalid, remove the queen (backtrack) and try the next position. Prune invalid placements early.

7. Given a stream of numbers, find the median at each insertion.

Station 10: The Trophy Shelf (Heap) -- Two heaps: a max-heap for the lower half and a min-heap for the upper half. The trophies on top of each shelf give you the median.

8. Given an array of temperatures, for each day find how many days until a warmer temperature.

Station 11: The Plate Stack (Monotonic Stack) -- Stack days in decreasing temperature order. When a warmer day arrives, pop all cooler days (plates get crushed) and record the distance.

9. Given a weighted graph, find the shortest path from source to all other nodes (no negative edges).

Station 16: The GPS Device (Dijkstra) -- The GPS processes the nearest unvisited node and updates distances. "Recalculating..." when a shorter path is found.

10. Given a list of words, implement autocomplete that returns all words with a given prefix.

Station 13: Phone Tree Chart (Trie) -- Follow the path letter by letter. At the prefix node, all descendants are valid completions. The tree fans out like a phone tree.
