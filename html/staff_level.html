What Makes a Staff Engineer

Beyond Writing Code

The difference between senior and staff is not technical depth -- it is technical breadth, judgment, and influence. A senior engineer delivers features. A staff engineer shapes the technical direction of a team or organization. This guide covers the non-coding skills that define the role.

Part 1: The Staff Engineer's Job

1.1 The Four Archetypes (from Will Larson)

Why "Tech Lead / Staff / Principal"?

The engineering ladder names reflect scope of influence. Tech Lead comes from "technical leadership" -- you lead a team's technical direction (like a lead engineer on a circuit board design). Staff is borrowed from military rank ("staff officer") -- someone who works across an organization advising on strategy rather than commanding a single unit. Principal means "first" or "chief" (from Latin principalis) -- the principal engineer is the foremost technical authority, like a principal investigator in research. The progression is deliberate: team scope, org scope, company scope.

Staff Engineer Archetypes

You do not need to fit one perfectly, but understanding which one your role emphasizes helps you focus your energy.

1.2 The Staff Engineer's Core Outputs

  1. Technical strategy documents -- Where are we going and why?
  2. Design documents (RFCs) -- How are we building this specific thing?
  3. Unblocking others -- Review, mentorship, cross-team coordination
  4. Quality standards -- Code review norms, testing expectations, architecture patterns
  5. Risk identification -- "This will break at 10x scale because..."
Key Insight

You are measured by organizational impact, not by lines of code.

Part 2: Writing Design Documents (RFCs)

Why "RFC"?

RFC stands for Request for Comments. The name originated at ARPANET in 1969 when Steve Crocker, a 26-year-old grad student, needed to document early internet protocol ideas. He titled his notes "Request for Comments" deliberately to sound non-authoritative and invite discussion -- he was junior and did not want to appear to be dictating standards. The humble name stuck, and RFCs became the official way internet standards are published to this day (TCP is RFC 793, HTTP/1.1 is RFC 2616). In companies, the name carries the same intent: an RFC is a formal proposal that requests feedback from peers before you commit to building. The name itself is a reminder -- you are asking for comments, not issuing orders.

This is the most important staff-level skill that most engineers never practice. A design doc is the equivalent of a schematic review in hardware -- it is how you get alignment before building.

**Author**: [Your name]
**Status**: Draft | In Review | Approved | Rejected
**Date**: [Date]

## Context

What is the problem? Why does it need to be solved now?
What happens if we do nothing?

## Goals and Non-Goals

**Goals**: What this design will accomplish.
**Non-goals**: What this design explicitly will NOT address
              (prevents scope creep).

## Proposal

The actual design. Include:

- High-level architecture diagram
- Key components and their responsibilities
- Data model / schema
- API contracts
- Error handling strategy

## Alternatives Considered

What other approaches did you evaluate? Why did you
reject them? This section proves you thought broadly
before deciding.

## Risks and Mitigations

What could go wrong? How will you detect it?
What is the rollback plan?

## Milestones

Phase 1: [scope] -- [timeline]
Phase 2: [scope] -- [timeline]

## Open Questions

Things you need input on from reviewers.

2.3 What Makes a Great Design Doc

  • Concise: 3-6 pages. If it is longer, the design is too complex or you are over-specifying implementation details.
  • Alternatives section is substantive: Not "we could do nothing" -- real alternatives with honest tradeoff analysis.
  • Addresses failure modes: What happens when the database is down? When traffic spikes 10x? When a dependency changes its API?
  • Clear on what is NOT included: Non-goals prevent scope creep.

2.4 Practice

Write a design doc for one of these (real or hypothetical):

  1. Migrating a service from monolith to microservices
  2. Adding caching to a read-heavy API
  3. Designing a feature flag system
  4. Replacing a legacy data store with a new one
Tip

Share it with a trusted colleague for review. The practice is in receiving feedback on your design thinking, not just the writing.

Part 3: Making Technical Decisions

3.1 The Decision Framework

For every significant technical decision, evaluate:

Dimension Questions
Correctness Does it solve the actual problem?
Complexity How much complexity does it add? Can the team maintain it?
Cost Build time, infrastructure cost, operational overhead?
Reversibility Is this a one-way door or a two-way door?
Timeline Does it fit the business timeline?

One-Way vs Two-Way Doors (Amazon concept)

⚠
One-Way Door
Hard to reverse. Invest time in the decision.
  • Database choice
  • API published to external clients
  • Programming language
✔
Two-Way Door
Easy to reverse. Decide quickly and iterate.
  • Internal library choice
  • Feature flag
  • Algorithm implementation

3.2 Build vs Buy vs Adopt

Option When Risk
Build Core competitive advantage, unique requirements, nothing fits Maintenance burden, time to build
Buy (SaaS/managed) Non-core, well-solved problem, team is small Vendor lock-in, cost at scale
Adopt (open source) Good fit exists, community is healthy Maintenance if community dies, security
Rule

Build only what differentiates your product. Buy/adopt everything else.

3.3 Communicating Decisions

After making a decision, document:

  • What was decided
  • Why (the reasoning, not just the conclusion)
  • What alternatives were considered and rejected
  • When to revisit (conditions under which the decision should be reconsidered)

This is an ADR (Architecture Decision Record). Keep them in a known location (wiki, repo). Future engineers will thank you.

Why "ADR"?

ADR stands for Architecture Decision Record. The name is literal: it records a decision about architecture. Think of it like a lab notebook entry for engineering decisions -- it captures not just what you decided, but the context at the time, what options you considered, and why you chose one over the others. The format was popularized by Michael Nygard in 2011. The key insight in the name is "Record" -- it exists so that future engineers (including future you) can understand why a decision was made, not just what was decided. Without ADRs, teams endlessly re-debate settled decisions because nobody remembers the original reasoning.

Part 4: Cross-Team Influence

4.1 Working Without Authority

Staff engineers rarely have direct authority over people. You influence through:

  • Expertise: People follow your lead because your technical judgment is sound
  • Relationships: You have earned trust across teams through consistent, helpful interactions
  • Documentation: Your RFCs and ADRs create institutional knowledge
  • Mentorship: You help others grow, and they amplify your impact

4.2 Navigating Disagreements

When you disagree with another senior/staff engineer:

  1. Assume good intent: They have information or constraints you do not see
  2. Make it about the problem, not the person: "This approach has risk X" not "Your approach is wrong"
  3. Write it down: Verbal arguments go in circles. Written pros/cons converge
  4. Propose an experiment: "Can we prototype both and measure?"
  5. Disagree and commit: Once a decision is made, support it fully even if you would have chosen differently

4.3 Mentoring Engineers

As a staff engineer, you are expected to grow other engineers. Effective mentoring:

  • Ask questions instead of giving answers: "What happens if this fails?" teaches more than "Add error handling here."
  • Review their designs, not just their code: Design review is higher leverage than code review.
  • Share your reasoning, not just your conclusions: "I chose X because of Y tradeoff" teaches decision-making.
  • Give them problems slightly above their level: Growth happens at the edge of comfort.

Part 5: Observability and Operations

Why "Observability"?

The term comes from control theory, introduced by Hungarian-American engineer Rudolf Kalman in 1960 (yes, the same Kalman behind the Kalman filter used in everything from GPS to spacecraft navigation). In control theory, a system is observable if you can determine its complete internal state solely from its external outputs. Software borrowed this concept directly: a system has good observability if you can understand what is happening inside it -- even in failure modes you never anticipated -- just by examining its logs, metrics, and traces. The distinction matters: monitoring tells you when something is broken (predefined checks), but observability lets you ask why it is broken (open-ended exploration). For an EE, think of it this way: monitoring is like having warning LEDs on a board, observability is like having full test points on every signal path.

Staff engineers own the operational health of their systems. You must understand:

5.1 The Three Pillars of Observability

Three Pillars of Observability
Pillar What it tells you EE Analogy
Logs What happened (discrete events) Logic analyzer trace
Metrics How is the system performing over time (aggregates) Oscilloscope -- signal levels, frequency response
Traces How did a single request flow through the system Signal path analysis across multiple boards

❌

▲

Why "SLO / SLI / SLA"?

These three terms are named in escalating order of commitment. SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the raw measurement -- a metric like "99.2% of requests completed in under 200ms." It indicates how the service is performing. SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target you set internally -- "We aim for 99.5% of requests under 200ms." It is your objective, your engineering goal. SLA (Service Level Agreement) is a contractual promise to customers with consequences -- "We guarantee 99.9% uptime or we pay penalties." It is a legal agreement. The naming progression is deliberate: indicator (what you measure) -> objective (what you aim for) -> agreement (what you promise). In EE terms: SLI is the oscilloscope reading, SLO is the design spec, SLA is the datasheet guarantee.

Why "MTTR / MTTF / MTBF"?

These reliability metrics are named after what they measure in the failure lifecycle. MTTF (Mean Time To Failure) -- average time a system runs before it fails. MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery) -- average time to restore service after a failure. MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) -- the full cycle: MTBF = MTTF + MTTR. These originated in hardware reliability engineering (MIL-HDBK-217, the military handbook for predicting electronic equipment reliability), so they should feel familiar from an EE background. The "Mean" in each name is important -- these are statistical averages, not guarantees. A system with 99.99% availability has about 52 minutes of downtime per year; MTTR tells you whether that is one 52-minute outage or fifty-two 1-minute blips.

5.3 Incident Response

Why "Incident Response"?

The term is borrowed directly from emergency services -- fire departments, paramedics, and law enforcement all use "incident response" to describe their structured process for handling emergencies. The software industry adopted both the term and the framework: designated responders, severity levels, communication protocols, command structure (Incident Commander), and post-incident reviews. Even "postmortem" is borrowed from medicine (Latin: "after death" -- examining what went wrong after the fact). The military and emergency services influence is deliberate: when production is down and users are affected, you need the same disciplined, rehearsed response as a fire crew, not ad-hoc scrambling.

Incident Response Timeline
Critical Rule

Mitigate first, investigate second. The hospital analogy: stop the bleeding before diagnosing the disease.

When production breaks:

  1. Detect: Alerts fire (automated monitoring)
  2. Triage: Is this impacting users? How many? What is the blast radius?
  3. Mitigate: Restore service ASAP (rollback, feature flag, scale up)
  4. Root cause: AFTER mitigation, investigate why
  5. Postmortem: Blameless writeup of what happened, timeline, and action items
